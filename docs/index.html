Watt's Mine Is Yours

A community energy proposal by Team Cul-de-Watt: Jasper de Wolfe de Wytt, 17 October 2026. ComErgy Grand Challenge 2026.

Nine Sydney households share their solar panels and batteries with a simple rule, and split the saving so that every one of them pays less and, in the benchmark year, no group would have done better by leaving. Their electricity costs fall by 39%, their peak-price purchases by 85% and their grid purchases by 41%.

The community

Nine households in Sydney make up the community. Between them they own 49.28 kW of solar panels and 65.24 kWh of batteries, but not evenly. Member 5, a single parent on the lowest income, owns neither. Members 2 and 7 have batteries but no panels to charge them.

How it works

Every hour the same four steps run, in this order.

  1. Own solar first Each home uses its own panels' output.
  2. Then neighbours Spare solar goes to homes that need it, including homes with no panels.
  3. Then batteries What is still spare charges the batteries, which later supply any home.
  4. Then the grid The grid covers the rest and takes any solar nobody can use.

Monthly bills

Grid electricity costs the retail price. Electricity from a neighbour costs the midpoint between the retail price and the feed-in tariff (what the grid pays for exported solar): $0.10, $0.15 or $0.30 per kWh for off-peak, in-between (shoulder) and peak hours. Buyer and seller both do better than with the grid.

One true-up a year

A single settling-up payment in or out, the true-up, moves each household onto its share of the saving. Member 5's monthly bills add up to $1,645; the true-up refunds $229. The payments always sum to zero.

The even split

The shares make the smallest gain any group of households gets from staying, compared with running its own equipment, as large as possible. No income data is needed.

Prices are in Australian dollars. The benchmark labels them in cents, but as cents they would be about a hundred times below any Australian tariff, and the benchmark names the Australian dollar as its currency.

In summer, local solar runs every home from mid-morning, the batteries fill, and they carry the homes through the peak-price hours and into the night. In winter the evenings are longer and colder, so the batteries cover the peak and then run out, and the grid supplies the night. Every household's bill

Together the nine homes pay $7,499 a year instead of $12,292, 39% less. Every household pays less than on its own; the smallest saving is $99. Member 5 saves $822, and the share of its income spent on electricity falls from 3.2% to 2.0%, the largest cut of any household. Member 7, whose battery had no panels to charge it, saves the most, $1,749.

Nobody wants to leave, in the year we tested

Any household could leave and run its own equipment for itself, and so could any group of households. With nine homes there are 510 such groups. We simulated the benchmark year for every one of them, let each choose the cheapest of five simple ways to run its own panels and batteries, and compared that with what its members pay together in the community.

Under the even split, even the group closest to leaving pays about $20 a year less inside than it would have paid on its own, and every household ends at least $99 ahead of its own best alternative. Give every household an equal gain over its best alternative instead and 182 groups would be better off on their own: the grey line drops below zero. The result holds for this year, these tariffs and these five rules; other years, prices and controllers have not been tested.

Sharing saves about $4,792 a year overall, but only about $25 a year of additional community-only running costs can be absorbed before some group could save more by leaving: the community could cost up to $7,524 a year and costs $7,499. Those costs must therefore be established before households join.

One harder test fails. If breakaway groups could foresee the whole year's weather and demand and plan their batteries perfectly, some would beat the community and the money would fall $469 short of keeping them all in. Nobody has that foresight, the community included, so we treat it as a stress test.

Grid and climate

Local solar now meets 62% of the community's use, up from 35%. The biggest change comes when electricity is dearest: the batteries carry the homes through the peak-price hours on most weekdays. Emissions here are those attributed to the community's grid purchases: purchases times the 2025 National Greenhouse Accounts location-based factor for New South Wales, 0.64 kg CO₂-e per kWh. The community also exports 36% less solar that other customers would have used, so this is not a claim about the whole power system.
